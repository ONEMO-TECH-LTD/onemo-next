> Written for Dan in product language. Zero technical jargon.

## Part 1: What the UX Chain Says Create Must Be

The UX chain is a 14-step specification generated by agents. It defines what the Create module should do, screen by screen, feature by feature. Here's what it actually says, translated from the academic language into real product behavior.

### The Nine Features It Demands

**Feature 1 — Getting the artwork in and seeing it on the product for the first time**

The customer arrives at Create. They pick an image (upload from phone at launch — AI generation, imports, etc. later). The system checks if the image is usable. If it is, the image appears on a 3D Effect immediately. This is the "first credible value" moment — the customer sees their artwork on a real-looking product for the first time.

The UX says this should happen on a dedicated intake screen (`/create`) BEFORE a draft even exists. The draft is only created after the customer sees and accepts the first preview. If the image isn't suitable (too small, wrong format), the system tells them why and lets them try again.

**Feature 2 — Saving your work and coming back later**

Everything the customer does is automatically saved. They can close the browser, come back a week later, and pick up exactly where they left off — same design, same stage, same choices.

This works even without an account. Anonymous users get continuity. When they later create an account, everything merges seamlessly. No lost work, ever.

The UX specifies that the system keeps a "last known good state" — if something goes wrong during a session break, it can always restore the customer to their last coherent moment.

**Feature 3 — Editing the design: positioning artwork and choosing materials**

This is the core creative loop. Two activities happen here:

*Composition* — dragging artwork around on the 3D product, resizing it, rotating it. The customer touches the 3D surface directly and moves their image. Alongside the gesture controls, there are always numeric inputs (position, scale, rotation) for precision and accessibility.

*Configuration* — choosing the product options: size, face material (ultra suede, velvet, gloss, etc.), trim/back color, subtype (edge trim, plain, TV retro). Every change updates the 3D preview in real time.

The UX says these are two separate "modes" — a composition mode for placing artwork and a configuration mode for material/color/size. The customer switches between them quickly inside the same draft.

It also demands a "known-good snapshot" concept: before experimenting with a big change, the system saves the current state. If the experiment doesn't work out, the customer can restore to the known-good version without losing everything.

**Feature 4 — Reviewing and trusting the preview before buying**

This is the proof gate. The customer moves from editing to reviewing. The review screen shows a controlled preview of exactly what will be manufactured. The customer inspects it and makes a decision: trust it and proceed, or go back and revise.

The UX says the preview must be "trustworthy" — it must accurately represent what the physical product will look like. If the preview can't be generated (loading issue, degraded quality), the system must say so explicitly rather than showing a low-quality image and pretending it's fine.

If the customer doesn't trust the preview, they go back to editing with a clear description of what didn't look right ("the color seems off" or "the placement moved"). This revise reason survives the round-trip so they don't forget what they wanted to fix.

**Feature 5 — Being ready to buy or share**

After the customer trusts the preview and approves it, the system checks: is everything actually ready? Are the selected options still available? Is the design still valid?

If something has changed (a variant went out of stock, the product spec updated), the system catches it here and tells the customer what to fix — not at checkout where it would be frustrating.

This is where bundle suggestions appear: "You're buying a single Effect — would you also like a compatible T-shirt to put it on?" Suggestions are always optional, never forced.

**Feature 6 — Sharing privately for feedback**

Before buying, the customer can share their design with specific people — a friend, a partner, a teammate. The recipient sees a clean preview and can leave feedback. The customer reads the feedback and decides: proceed to buy, revise the design, or wait.

The share link is private by default. It doesn't make the design public. The customer controls who sees it.

**Feature 7 — Public presentation (stretch goal)**

Optionally, the customer can publish their design to a public ONEMO page. This is for social sharing, discovery, and wider audience reach. Others can view it, start a conversation, buy their own copy, or remix it.

This feature is marked "stretch" — not required for MVP.

**Feature 8 — Feeling like you made something real**

After finishing the design, the UX wants the customer to feel the result is personally authored — not a template fill, not a merch printout. This is the emotional payoff of the creative journey.

The UX models this as a formal recognition moment: the system surfaces the meaning of what was created. If the result still feels generic, it suggests going back to make it more personal.

**Feature 9 — Building a collection over time (post-MVP)**

Across multiple sessions, the customer builds a set of Effects. The system tracks coherence across the collection — do these pieces express a consistent identity? It suggests next directions to strengthen the set.

This is explicitly post-MVP. It's noted so the architecture doesn't block it later.

### The 14 Things It Says the System Must Track

The UX defines 14 "content model objects" — the pieces of information the system needs to manage. Here they are in plain language:

1. **Artwork source record** — Where did the image come from? Upload, AI generation, library pick, or remix? What's its status — uploaded successfully, processing, failed? This exists BEFORE a draft is created.

2. **Draft record** — The canonical "this design exists" record. Has an ID, knows what stage it's at (editing, reviewing, shared, purchased), links to the artwork source and the current design choices.

3. **Design workspace state** — The actual creative choices: where the artwork is placed, what material is selected, what color, what size. Also holds the "known-good snapshot" for experiment/restore.

4. **Commerce variant context** — The Shopify product/variant information. What sizes are available? What's in stock? This is owned by Shopify, not by ONEMO. The configurator reads it but doesn't control it.

5. **Preview snapshot** — The current visual representation of the design. This is derived from the design state, not canonical. It can lag behind (if the preview is still generating) or be degraded (if the system is under load). The UX insists this must be explicitly stated — never pretend a stale preview is current.

6. **Proof record** — When the customer enters review, this tracks: did they inspect the preview? Do they trust it? If not, what specifically don't they trust? What's their decision — proceed, revise, or hold?

7. **Resume checkpoint** — The last coherent state the system can restore after a session break. Points to the draft and design state that were confirmed before the interruption.

8. **Readiness check** — After approval: are there any remaining blockers? Is the variant still available? Is the approval still fresh (not expired)? What path did the customer choose — buy now, or publish publicly first?

9. **Private share context** — Who was the share sent to? What was the purpose? What feedback came back? What did the customer decide based on the feedback?

10. **Public presentation context** — The public page for the design. Publication state, framing/story, audience next steps (view, buy, remix).

11. **Commerce handoff** — The package handed to Shopify when the customer clicks buy. Contains the variant ID, the design reference, line-item properties for cart display, and pair/bundle metadata if applicable.

12. **Authored outcome feeling** — Does the customer feel the result is personally theirs? Does it feel authored or generic? If generic, what's missing?

13. **Identity set context** — How does this design fit into the customer's broader collection? Style coherence, next direction suggestions.

14. **Action safety guard** — For every action that takes time (uploading, generating a preview, saving, sharing), this tracks: is it in progress? Did it succeed? Did it fail? Can the customer retry? This prevents double-submits and silent failures.

### The Screens and Navigation It Prescribes

The UX defines exactly where the customer goes:

- `/create` — **Intake screen.** Choose image, upload, see first preview. No draft exists yet.
- `/create/{draftId}` — **Draft shell.** The main workspace. Everything happens here.
- `?mode=compose` — **Placement mode.** Drag artwork, resize, rotate on the 3D surface.
- `?mode=configure` — **Options mode.** Choose material, color, size, subtype.
- `/create/{draftId}/review` — **Proof screen.** Inspect the authoritative preview. Approve or revise.
- `/share/private/{ref}` — **Private share page.** Recipient views the design, leaves feedback.
- `/presentation/{ref}` — **Public page.** Anyone can view, buy, or remix.
- **Recovery overlay** — When something goes wrong (upload failed, preview stale), a pop-up explains what happened and how to fix it without leaving the current screen.
- **Action safety state** — Tracks all pending/completed/failed async operations invisibly.
- **Resume state** — Restores the last good state after a session break.

### What the MVP Skeleton Keeps vs Defers

The UX itself says not everything is needed at launch:

**MVP keeps (the minimum lovable path):**
- Intake → upload → see first preview → accept into draft
- One meaningful edit (place artwork) and one configuration (pick material/color)
- Proof review with trust check and revise loop
- Same-draft resume after session break
- One recovery path (when upload or preview fails)

**MVP defers:**
- Readiness/time-sensitive purchase flow (Feature 5)
- Private sharing (Feature 6)
- Public presentation (Feature 7)
- Authored outcome recognition (Feature 8)
- Identity set coherence (Feature 9)
- All commerce handoff detail

## Part 2: What's Wrong With It

Now that you know what the UX chain says, here's where it goes off the rails.

### Where it overengineered simple things into academic systems

**1. The "proof record" (item 6) is a form with one checkbox.**

The UX created a formal domain object with fields like `trust_verdict`, `inspected_view_refs`, `trusted_other_context`, `revise_gap_summary`, and `decision_state`. In reality, this is: "Does the preview look good? Yes → proceed. No → what's wrong?" That's a boolean plus an optional text field on the review screen. Not a separate tracked entity with its own lifecycle.

**2. The "readiness check" (item 8) manufactures urgency that the brand explicitly bans.**

The UX models an "opportunity readiness frame" with fields like `opportunity_context` (time-sensitive purchase window) and `remaining_blockers`. But the brand guidelines say: "No urgency tactics. No timers. No 'almost sold out' pressure." This object's entire purpose is to model time pressure — which contradicts the product values. The simpler version: before checkout, check if the variant is still available and the approval hasn't expired. That's a validation step, not a domain object.

**3. The "authored outcome feeling" (item 12) turns an emotion into a database record.**

Fields: `authored_verdict`, `meaning_summary`, `self_expression_readiness`, `missing_expression_gap`. This is trying to quantify whether the customer feels personally connected to their creation. You can't put "does this feel like mine?" in a database. This should be a UX quality measure (does the design flow make people feel creative?), not a tracked entity.

**4. The "identity set" (item 13) is post-MVP catalog logic pretending to be Create.**

Building a coherent collection over time is a Library/Profile feature, not a Create feature. The UX placed it inside Create's domain model because it was generated from stories about the "Collector" persona. But Create's job ends when you finish one design. Managing collections across designs is a different module's responsibility.

**5. The "known-good snapshot plus live experiment" pattern is premature.**

The UX specifies that before trying a new material or color, the system takes a snapshot of the current state. If the experiment doesn't work, the customer restores from the snapshot. For MVP, undo (go back to previous state) achieves the same thing with much less complexity. The formal experiment/restore model is useful for a mature product with complex multi-step experiments — not for an MVP where "undo" is sufficient.

### Where it missed real product needs

**1. No loading experience defined.**

When the customer first opens `/create`, what do they see while the 3D scene loads? The UX describes the intake screen as a destination but never addresses the 2-6 seconds where the engine is initializing. This is a real moment that needs design.

**2. Caps and pairs don't have 3D scenes.**

The UX demands cap-specific geometry and pair visualization. But no scene presets, GLB models, or Studio authoring workflows exist for caps or pairs. You can't configure a cap if there's no 3D cap model to render.

**3. Bundle suggestion behavior is a sentence, not a design.**

The delta supplement says "suggestions appear in the review screen as secondary content." Where exactly? How do they look? What happens when you accept one? How does it affect the cart? This is handwaved.

**4. Attachment messaging has no copy.**

The UX says "keep messaging consistent across all views" for the attachment system. But there's no message catalog. No copy for "this Effect uses magnetic attachment — you'll need a compatible garment." The UX demands consistency of something that doesn't exist yet.

**5. The UX doesn't know about the actual 3D technology.**

The original UX chain was generated before the decision to use 3D surface gestures. A separate "delta supplement" had to patch in: 3D gestures, numeric precision assist, gesture-to-canonical reconciliation, safe-area enforcement, and back-side visualization. These patches work but they feel bolted on rather than native to the specification.

### Where it got things right

- **The routing structure** — intake → draft → compose/configure → review. Clean and correct.
- **Source-agnostic intake** — upload is first, but the pipeline accepts future sources. Well-designed.
- **The proof gate** — you must trust the preview before buying. This is the right boundary.
- **Commerce separation** — Shopify never owns design truth. Design hands off to commerce, not the other way around.
- **Anonymous start + seamless account upgrade** — create without logging in, everything merges when you do.
- **Autosave + resume** — save on every meaningful change, restore exactly where you left off.
- **Action safety** — preventing double-submits and showing clear pending/success/failed states for every async operation. This pattern is genuinely important and correctly specified.
- **Private-first approach** — designs are private by default. Public sharing is an explicit escalation.

## Part 3: What Can Start Now vs What Needs Your Call

### Build now (no UX dependency)

These are pure infrastructure and domain contracts. The UX doesn't influence them:

- **Domain schemas** — ProductSpec, ScenePreset, DesignSession structures. Defined by the product catalog and requirements, not by UX.
- **Repository pattern** — file-backed for development, Supabase for production. Same interface, swappable.
- **Scene loading** — load .onemo files, validate integrity, parse into renderable objects.
- **Viewer shell extraction** — take the working prototype viewer and make it reusable.
- **Product module registry** — the plug-in system that lets new product types register without changing the core viewer.
- **Compatibility rules engine** — the centralized set of rules that says "this configuration is valid" or "you need a compatible garment."
- **Autosave mechanism** — debounce changes, save to server, append revision snapshot.
- **Design revision history** — every save creates an immutable snapshot for proof and manufacturing.

### Needs your validation before building

| Decision | What it affects | Options |
|----------|----------------|---------|
| **Is there a separate intake screen before the draft?** | The very first screen. Do customers upload on a dedicated page, then get redirected to the draft? Or do they land directly in the draft shell and upload there? | A: Separate intake screen (UX proposal) — cleaner but adds a transition. B: Upload inside the draft shell — simpler, one less screen. |
| **Two modes or one workspace?** | How editing works. Are "place artwork" and "choose material/color" separate modes you switch between? Or one workspace with all controls visible? | A: Two modes (UX proposal) — focused, less visual noise per mode. B: One workspace — everything accessible, no mode switching. |
| **Separate review screen or panel within the draft?** | Where proof happens. Is review a whole new page with its own navigation? Or a slide-out panel within the editing screen? | A: Separate screen (UX proposal) — clear commitment boundary. B: Panel within draft — faster, less navigation. |
| **Formal experiment/restore or simple undo?** | How "trying a new color and going back" works. Does the system take a formal snapshot before experiments? Or just use undo/redo? | A: Formal snapshot (UX proposal) — explicit, but complex. B: Undo/redo via revision history — simpler, covers 90% of cases. |
| **Should items 6, 8, 12 be domain objects or UI states?** | Whether the "proof record," "authored feeling," and "readiness check" are real database entities or just UI-level display states. | A: Real objects (UX proposal) — trackable, queryable, but overengineered for MVP. B: UI states — simpler, sufficient for launch. |
| **Bundle suggestion design?** | Where and how "you might also want a T-shirt" appears. | Needs actual UX design — the current spec is too vague. |

## Part 4: Recommended Sequence

**Phase 0-1 (start now):** Schemas, viewer, scene loading, product modules, compatibility engine, fallback strategy. Zero UX dependency. All infrastructure.

**Phase 2 (after your validation):** The actual customer-facing edit loop. Needs the 6 decisions above before building screens.

**Phase 3+ (after Phase 2 is working):** Proof, manufacturing handoff, commerce, sharing, AI. Commerce also needs the operations folder finished (returns policy, fulfillment SLAs, shipping — the work Gem just delivered on).
